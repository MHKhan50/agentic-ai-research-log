\begin{abstract}
Small language models that run on a clinic's own computer are attractive for drafting clinical notes, because patient speech never leaves the building. In Pakistan and in UK clinics serving Pakistani communities, many consultations are held in Roman Urdu: Urdu written in Latin script and mixed with English clinical terms. We ask whether two small local models, Qwen2.5 3B and Llama 3.1 8B, write English SOAP notes from Roman Urdu consultations as safely as from English ones. We wrote 20 fictional physiotherapy consultations, each in English and Roman Urdu with identical facts, listed 168 facts (68 critical) and checked every note automatically for omissions, wrong body regions and the injured side. From Roman Urdu, Llama 3.1 8B omitted 16 of 168 facts against 1 from English (exact McNemar $p=0.0003$) and Qwen2.5 3B omitted 50 against 6 ($p<0.0001$). Critical facts were lost only in Roman Urdu (8 and 26). The injured side was missing from 13 and 14 of the 14 Roman Urdu notes where a side applied, and reading the notes showed a knee replacement rewritten as a hip replacement, a hip as the lower back, and left as right. In a four-case pilot, translating into English before drafting did not help. A simple rule-based gate flagged 17 of 20 Llama 3.1 8B Roman Urdu notes and none of its English notes. A blinded review of 50 notes by a panel of five physiotherapists agreed with the automatic side check (Cohen's $\kappa=0.88$) and body-region check ($\kappa=0.75$) and with the gate on 90\% of notes, but not with the keyword check for critical facts ($\kappa=0.12$, 17 false alarms); the panel also found invented or wrong details in 35 of the 40 Roman Urdu notes. All consultations, notes and code are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large language models can turn a consultation into a structured note, but they also leave facts out and add facts that were never said. \citet{asgari2025framework} measured this for GPT-4 on 450 simulated UK primary-care consultations and found a 1.47\% hallucination rate and a 3.45\% omission rate, with most major errors in the Plan. Their study used one large hosted model and English only.

Two practical settings fall outside that study. First, many clinics cannot send recordings to a hosted model, so the relevant models are small ones that run locally. Second, a large share of consultations in Pakistan, and among Pakistani patients in the UK, take place in Roman Urdu, which is rarely represented in clinical evaluation data.

This paper reports a controlled comparison: the same consultations, the same facts and the same prompt, once in English and once in Roman Urdu, drafted by two small local models. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a public set of 20 paired English and Roman Urdu physiotherapy consultations with 168 listed facts, 68 marked critical, including deliberate red flags;
  \item a paired comparison showing that both models lose significantly more facts, and only lose critical facts, in Roman Urdu;
  \item an analysis of anatomy and laterality errors, showing that the injured side is almost never carried into the English note;
  \item a negative result for translating first, and a simple rule-based gate that routes notes to a clinician;
  \item a blinded clinical review of 50 notes that validates the side and body-region checks and shows where keyword checks fail.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Clinical note generation.} Drafting a visit note from a doctor and patient conversation has public English benchmarks such as ACI-BENCH \citep{yim2023acibench}, which pairs visit dialogues with notes and reports results for several systems. \citet{asgari2025framework} moved from overlap scores to clinician labels of each hallucinated or omitted sentence and graded them by harm. We follow their idea of counting omissions and harmful errors, but replace sentence labelling by a fixed list of facts per consultation, so that the same facts can be checked in both languages.

\paragraph{Code-switched language.} Benchmarks such as GLUECoS \citep{khanuja2020gluecos} show that models trained mostly on one language lose accuracy on text that mixes two. Roman Urdu with English clinical terms is this kind of text, and it is also written without a fixed spelling. To our knowledge no public clinical benchmark covers it.

\paragraph{Translating first.} A common way to handle a low-resource input is to translate it into English and then run the English system. \citet{artetxe2023revisiting} found that this translate-test approach can be stronger than earlier work suggested when the translation system is good. Our pilot tests the same idea with the small drafting model doing the translation itself.

\section{Data and method}
\paragraph{Consultations.} The author wrote 20 short fictional physiotherapy consultations (back, neck, shoulder, knee, hip and pelvis, ankle and foot, wrist and hand, elbow). Each exists in English and Roman Urdu with the same facts and numbers. Clinical terms that clinicians in Pakistan normally say in English (for example ligament, ACL, scaphoid) stay in English inside the Roman Urdu, as in real code-mixed speech. Several cases contain red flags on purpose: saddle numbness with urinary difficulty (suspected cauda equina syndrome), a swollen calf two weeks after knee replacement (possible deep vein thrombosis), weight loss with a history of cancer, and a possible scaphoid fracture in a patient on warfarin. No real patient data was used.

\paragraph{Facts.} Each consultation lists 6 to 11 facts with a keyword pattern; 68 of 168 are marked critical (red-flag answers, medicines, referrals and urgent actions). Every pattern is tested to match its own English transcript. Fourteen cases have an injured side.

\paragraph{Models and prompt.} Qwen2.5 3B \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and Llama 3.1 8B \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} ran through Ollama on a MacBook M4 with 16\,GB memory, at temperature 0 with a fixed seed. The system prompt asked for a concise English SOAP note with four headings, using only information in the transcript. This gives 80 notes.

\paragraph{Checks.} For each note we record (i) listed facts whose pattern is absent, (ii) whether the main body region is named, (iii) body regions named in the note but absent from the English transcript, (iv) whether the injured side is named, and (v) whether the opposite side is named without support. A note is \emph{flagged} for clinician review if any of (ii) to (v) fails or a critical fact is missing.

\paragraph{Clinician review.} To test the automatic checks, we reviewed all 40 Roman Urdu notes and 10 English notes (5 per model, chosen at random with a fixed seed), shuffled and coded R01 to R50 so that the model, the input language and the automatic results were hidden. The English transcript was shown as the reference. A panel of five physiotherapists, led by M.K.\ (Doctor of Physical Therapy; MSc in Advanced Physiotherapy, Glasgow Caledonian University; about five years of clinical practice), read each note together and agreed one label per item: a wrong or missing body region, a wrong or missing side, a missing red flag or other critical fact, and any other error, such as an invented detail or a wrong number. M.K.\ then re-read every note on her own three to four times, revised labels where needed and wrote a comment for each note. The labels are therefore a single consensus judgement; agreement between individual clinicians was not measured. We report raw agreement and Cohen's kappa \citep{cohen1960kappa} between the panel's labels and each automatic check, and the precision and recall of each check with the panel's labels as the reference. The safety gate is compared with the panel's \emph{any error} label.

\paragraph{Statistics.} We report the facts-missing rate with a 95\% bootstrap interval that resamples whole consultations \citep{efron1979bootstrap}, and an exact two-sided McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947} on each fact paired across languages. Because facts from the same consultation are not independent, we also report an exact two-sided sign test at the consultation level, comparing the number of missing facts in the English and Roman Urdu note of each consultation. For Cohen's kappa we give 95\% bootstrap intervals over the 50 reviewed notes (2,000 resamples).

\paragraph{Omissions.} Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:main} show the main result. For Llama 3.1 8B, 16 facts were missing only in Roman Urdu and 1 only in English ($p=0.0003$); for critical facts the split was 8 to 0 ($p=0.0078$). For Qwen2.5 3B the splits were 47 to 3 and 26 to 0 (both $p<0.0001$). The larger model narrows the gap but does not close it. The result holds at the consultation level: the Roman Urdu note missed more facts than the English note in 11 of 20 consultations for Llama 3.1 8B and 18 of 20 for Qwen2.5 3B, and never fewer (sign test $p=0.001$ and $p<0.0001$).

\paragraph{Anatomy and laterality.} The main body region was wrong or missing in 6 Llama 3.1 8B and 11 Qwen2.5 3B Roman Urdu notes. The injured side was dropped almost always: \emph{daayan} (right) and \emph{baayan} (left) were not translated, and Llama 3.1 8B turned left into right twice. The knee replacement case shows why this matters. After knee surgery a swollen calf is the main warning sign of a deep vein thrombosis. The note still raised a possible clot and sent the patient to hospital, but with the wrong joint and without the sign that explains the concern.

\paragraph{Does translating first help?} On the first four consultations we also asked each model to translate the Roman Urdu into English and then draft from its own translation. Qwen2.5 3B returned the Roman Urdu unchanged in all four cases, so the step did nothing. For Llama 3.1 8B, critical facts missing rose from 3 (direct) to 5 (translate first) of 42 facts, and the translation itself swapped a left neck rotation to the right and turned a shoulder into the lower back. This pilot is small, but it gives no support for translation as a fix.

\paragraph{The gate.} The rule-based gate flagged 17 of 20 Llama 3.1 8B Roman Urdu notes and none of its English notes; 5 of the 17 were flagged only for a missing side. Two of the three Qwen2.5 3B English flags were fair wording rather than errors (``calf exercises'' for an ankle sprain, ``right leg'' for a right knee).

\paragraph{Clinician review.} Table~\ref{tab:review} compares the automatic checks with the panel's labels. The side check agreed on 47 of 50 notes ($\kappa=0.88$, 95\% CI 0.72--1.00) and raised no false alarm; the panel found three further side problems that the check missed. The body-region check agreed on 44 of 50 ($\kappa=0.75$, 0.53--0.92). The keyword check for critical facts did not agree with the panel ($\kappa=0.12$, $-0.12$--0.36): it flagged 25 notes, of which the panel confirmed 8, and missed 5 notes in which the panel found a missing critical fact. A keyword check counts a fact as missing whenever its pattern is absent, so a fact written in other words is flagged; we did not classify the 17 false alarms by cause. The gate, which combines all checks, agreed with the panel's \emph{any error} label on 45 of 50 notes ($\kappa=0.68$, 0.37--0.91); it flagged 39 notes, 38 of them correctly, and missed 4 of the 42 notes the panel judged wrong. The panel marked an invented or wrong detail (\emph{other error}) in 35 of the 40 Roman Urdu notes and in 2 of the 10 English notes; its comments describe invented ages and sexes, examination findings that were never performed, and changed numbers, such as a pain score of 5/10 read as pain lasting five hours.

\section{Discussion}
For a clinic using these models, the results point to three design rules. First, Roman Urdu notes from small local models cannot be used without a clinician reading the whole note. Second, the side and body region should be checked in code: if the transcript names a side, a note without one should be rejected or marked incomplete. Third, every step of a pipeline needs its own check, as the silent no-op translation shows. The clinical review supports the first two rules and qualifies the third: checks for side and body region agree well with clinicians and are suitable for routing notes to a clinician, although the gate still missed 4 of the 42 notes the panel judged wrong, but a keyword list of critical facts produces too many false alarms to stand alone, and none of the automatic checks looks for invented details, which the panel found in almost every Roman Urdu note.

\section{Limitations}
The consultations are fictional, written by one person, and short; real consultations are longer, noisier and come through speech recognition, which adds its own errors \citep{radford2023whisper}. Each model ran once at temperature 0. The checks are keyword based: a fact in unexpected words counts as missing, and a fact stated wrongly can still count as present. The clinical review covered 50 of the 80 notes and gives one consensus label per item, so agreement between clinicians was not measured, and the lead reviewer is a co-author and a member of the first author's family. Invented details were labelled only as a yes/no per note, not sentence by sentence. Roman Urdu spelling varies between speakers.
